BEYOND THE SDLC - Is the Software Engineer Becoming an Intent Architect?

We keep talking about AI as if its biggest impact on software development is that engineers will write code faster.

I think that misses the more important change.

AI is beginning to change who, or what, actually performs the software development lifecycle.

Today, most organizations are using AI to accelerate the existing SDLC. Developers use copilots. AI generates tests. Agents fix defects. Requirements get summarized. Documentation gets drafted. All useful. But that still assumes the lifecycle itself is fundamentally human-operated.

I believe we are moving toward something different: an AI-Augmented Development Lifecycle, or AI-ADLC, where humans increasingly define intent, constraints, architecture, and accountability while agents execute more of the lifecycle.

And if that happens, we may also need to rethink what we mean by software engineer.

Code may no longer be the bottleneck

Anthropic’s AI-native SDLC playbook makes an important observation: as AI accelerates implementation, the bottleneck moves toward planning, review and testing, and deployment. It also argues that traditional lifecycle controls assume humans perform every step, an assumption that becomes harder to sustain when agents generate much of a change.

Anthropic responds by rethinking six familiar stages: planning, design, building, testing, deploying, and maintaining, while continuing to emphasize human involvement.

That is an important step. But I believe the logical destination goes further. If humans are no longer doing most of the implementation, what should organize the lifecycle? My answer is intent, delegated authority, and evidence.

From AI assistance to AI agency

There is a meaningful difference between an AI assistant and an AI agent participating in a delivery system.

Assistant model: Human acts → AI helps → human acts

Agentic model: Human defines intent → agents execute → independent agents validate → policy evaluates evidence → humans handle exceptions

The second model requires more than a better coding assistant. It requires orchestration, explicit authority, governance, and evidence.

No agent should be the sole producer, validator, and authorizer of a consequential change.

Today’s separation of duties may need to evolve into separation of agency. A builder agent builds. An independent validator challenges the result. A policy or release authority evaluates the evidence. A runtime observer determines whether the result is behaving as intended.

That is very different from giving one highly capable agent access to everything.

Humans should own intent

So what are the humans doing? I think humans move up the abstraction stack.

  • Why we are solving the problem
  • What business outcome constitutes success
  • Which stakeholder needs must be balanced
  • Which constraints cannot be optimized away
  • What risk the organization is willing to accept
  • Where an agent’s authority begins and ends

Agents increasingly own how that intent is executed. That makes me wonder whether the most important engineering artifact in AI-ADLC will eventually stop being the code. Perhaps it will be the intent.

Organizations may need something closer to an intent contract: a durable representation of the desired outcome, business semantics, constraints, quality attributes, decision rights, escalation conditions, and evidence required to demonstrate success.

A prompt tells an AI what to do. An intent contract establishes the meaning and boundaries under which an agentic delivery system operates.

Humans still need to discover intent

I do not believe we are ready to hand this responsibility entirely to agents. Anyone who has worked closely with business partners knows that requirements rarely arrive fully formed.

People communicate through context. They leave important things unsaid. They make assumptions they do not know they are making. Two people use the same word to mean different things. Sometimes the most important requirement emerges from the conversation around the requirement.

AI can help identify ambiguity, expose contradictions, explore scenarios, and ask better questions. But in the near to mid term, humans still have an essential responsibility to uncover the subtleties of intent that other humans do not easily verbalize.

Is “software engineer” still the right description?

If agents eventually perform much of the implementation, testing, documentation, infrastructure configuration, and routine deployment, what defines the human engineering role? I do not think the answer is prompt engineer. That is far too narrow.

The role I see emerging combines:

  • Product management
  • Business and functional architecture
  • Systems architecture
  • Deep technical engineering
  • Governance of autonomous systems

The engineer moves closer to the business, helping uncover intent and translate it into something an agentic delivery system can reliably execute. I have started thinking of this role as an Intent Architect.

An Intent Architect works with the business to uncover desired outcomes and tacit meaning, translates them into governed and executable intent, establishes architectural and decision boundaries, and evaluates the evidence produced by agents executing within them.

That does not make technical depth less important. It may make it more important. An Intent Architect needs enough technical understanding to know when an agent-generated architecture is wrong, when its evidence is weak, when a shortcut creates unacceptable coupling, and when an apparently reasonable implementation closes off an important future option.

The abstraction level rises. The engineering judgment remains.

Governance cannot become the next bottleneck

If agents generate software at machine speed but every change waits for a human to approve it, we have not transformed the lifecycle. We have moved the bottleneck.

The answer, in my view, is graduated autonomy:

  • Green: Intent is clear, work remains inside approved architecture and policy, required controls pass, evidence is sufficient, and risk remains within tolerance. Agents proceed.
  • Yellow: Something is ambiguous, an exception is required, evidence is incomplete, or a risk threshold has been crossed. Agents recommend; humans decide.
  • Red: The decision changes policy, accepts material risk, changes a critical architectural boundary, or expands agent authority. Humans retain the decision.

This is where the NIST AI Risk Management Framework provides a useful complementary perspective. NIST describes a voluntary framework intended to incorporate trustworthiness considerations into the design, development, use, and evaluation of AI systems. Its companion playbook provides suggested actions for putting risk-management practices into operation.

Anthropic focuses on redesigning the software lifecycle around agentic capability. NIST supplies a broader risk-management lens. AI-ADLC needs both: a delivery model for agent execution and a governance model for deciding where autonomy is appropriate.

The important point is that decisions do not necessarily remain red forever. As controls, evidence, and organizational confidence improve, more decisions can safely migrate toward yellow and green.

Architecture becomes the paved road

Enterprise Architecture will have to evolve too. Architecture should not become a toll booth that every agent reaches before making a meaningful decision. Instead, architecture should create the paved road on which agents can move at machine speed.

Fast + reversible + inside established guardrails → agent decides

Exception with manageable consequences → agent recommends; human adjudicates

Hard to reverse + high impact + changes policy or risk → human decides

When humans keep approving the same exception, perhaps the architecture needs to change. Repeated decisions should become reusable policy, allowing the next agent to operate inside an expanded but still governed boundary.

Where this ultimately leads

I do not believe organizations jump directly from today’s copilots to autonomous development. I see a progression:

AI assistance → delegated agent tasks → orchestrated agent workflows → intent-driven delivery → human-governed autonomy → bounded closed-loop delivery

Anthropic offers a useful near-term blueprint for applying AI across the familiar lifecycle while retaining human involvement. NIST offers a complementary framework for incorporating trustworthiness into the design, development, use, and evaluation of AI systems.

My AI-ADLC hypothesis is about how those ideas converge as autonomy increases.

Human-in-the-loop everywhere should not necessarily be the destination. Human accountability should be.

Humans define purpose, policy, architecture, risk tolerance, and the boundaries of delegated authority. Independent agents and controls create evidence. Agents execute inside established boundaries. Humans return to the decision loop when meaning, ambiguity, risk, or consequence requires judgment.

And that leaves me with a question that may be bigger than the technology itself:

If the primary job is no longer writing the software, but understanding what the business needs, translating that into executable intent, establishing the boundaries within which AI can act, and governing the resulting system, are we still describing a software engineer?

Or are we beginning to describe something new?

I think we may be describing the Intent Architect.
